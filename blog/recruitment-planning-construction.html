Planning recruitment for construction projects

How to turn a project programme into a hiring plan, and the five numbers worth tracking.

By Navin Kumar Tiwary26 Oct 20266 min read

I have hired more than 600 people for construction and engineering projects, from labourers and foremen to engineers and office staff. The hiring that went smoothly always had one thing in common: we planned it before the project needed the people, not after.

Start from the project plan, not the job board

On a construction project, the programme tells you almost everything about recruitment. Before opening a single vacancy, I sit with the project team and turn the schedule into a manpower plan: how many people of each trade and grade are needed, and in which month.

Once you have this, you can see the peaks coming and start hiring in time.

Work backwards from the start date

The date someone is needed on site is not the date you should start looking. For an overseas hire you need time for sourcing, interviews, the offer, the work permit and visa, the medical, Emirates ID, travel, and induction. For a local transfer it is shorter, but still not instant.

I keep a realistic lead time for each category and count back from the start date. If the plan says 30 masons in month 3, the search for them begins in month 1.

Treat blue-collar and white-collar hiring differently

They are two different processes, and mixing them slows both down.

Blue-collar

  • Work with a small number of trusted recruitment agencies and judge them on quality, not just speed.
  • Use referrals from your best foremen. Good tradespeople usually know other good tradespeople.
  • Always include a practical trade test. A CV tells you very little about how someone fixes steel.
  • Check accommodation and transport capacity before people arrive, not after.

White-collar

  • Use LinkedIn and the regional job boards, and ask your own team for referrals.
  • Use structured interviews: the same core questions for every candidate, scored the same way.
  • Involve the hiring manager early so the job description matches what they really need.

Make the offer clear

Many candidates drop out at the offer stage because something was unclear. Spell out the basic salary and each allowance separately, because the basic is what gratuity and overtime are calculated on. Be clear about accommodation, transport, working hours, and the rest day. A clear offer avoids awkward conversations later.

Plan for people leaving, too

A manpower plan that only adds people is incomplete. Every month some people resign, some finish their contracts, and some go on annual leave. I add a replacement allowance to the plan based on last year's turnover, and I plan leave cover for key roles so a site does not lose its only foreman for a month.

Track a few numbers, not twenty

You don't need a complicated dashboard. These five numbers tell me most of what I need to know:

  • Time to fill: days from the approved requisition to the person joining.
  • Offer acceptance rate: how many offers are accepted.
  • No-show rate: people who accept but never join.
  • Early leavers: people who leave in the first 90 days.
  • Cost per hire: agency fees, visa costs and travel, by category.

If early leavers go up, look at the job description and the onboarding. If no-shows go up, look at the offer and how long the process takes.

The short version

  • Turn the project programme into a month-by-month manpower plan.
  • Start hiring early enough to cover visa and mobilisation time.
  • Run blue-collar and white-collar hiring as separate processes.
  • Make every offer clear, with basic and allowances shown separately.
  • Plan for turnover and leave cover, and track five simple numbers.

Good recruitment planning is not glamorous. It is mostly spreadsheets and conversations with project managers. But it is the difference between a site that starts on time and one that spends its first month waiting for people.
