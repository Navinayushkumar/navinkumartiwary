Performance review techniques that actually work

Six practical techniques, the biases to watch for, and how to review a large site workforce.

By Navin Kumar Tiwary26 Oct 20266 min read

Most people dread performance reviews, managers included. Usually that's because the review is the first time in a year anyone talks seriously about performance. These are the techniques I find make reviews fair, quick and actually useful.

Agree what "good" looks like at the start

A review can only be fair if the employee knew what they were being measured on. At the start of the year, or when someone joins, agree three to five goals that are specific to the role.

For a site supervisor that might be safety record, attendance of the team, and work completed against the programme. For a payroll officer it might be accuracy, meeting the monthly deadline, and clean reconciliations. Keep each goal clear enough that both sides would agree on whether it was met.

Six techniques worth knowing

1. Goal-based review

Each goal is reviewed one by one: met, partly met or not met, with the evidence. This works well for most office roles because the results are visible and easy to discuss.

2. Rating scales with clear descriptions

A 1 to 5 scale is only useful if everyone understands what a 3 means. Write a short description for each level, based on behaviour you can actually see. For example:

RatingMeaning (example: timekeeping and attendance)
5Never late, covers for others, sets the example for the team
4Reliable, very rarely late, always informs in advance
3Meets the standard, occasional lateness with a reason
2Regularly late or absent, needs reminders
1Attendance is affecting the work and needs formal action

3. Self-assessment

Ask the employee to rate themselves before the meeting. It shows you how they see their own work, and the conversation usually goes better when they have had time to think.

4. 360-degree feedback

Feedback from the manager, peers and the people they supervise. It takes more effort, so I would keep it for supervisors and managers, where how they treat people matters as much as what they deliver.

5. Regular check-ins

A short monthly or quarterly conversation about progress. When these happen, the annual review has no surprises and takes half the time.

6. Calibration

Before ratings are final, managers of similar teams sit together and compare. This catches the manager who gives everyone a 5 and the one who never gives more than a 3, so ratings mean the same thing across departments.

Watch out for the usual biases

Common biases

  • Recency: judging the whole year on the last month.
  • Halo: one strong point colours every other rating.
  • Central tendency: rating everyone as average to avoid difficult conversations.
  • Leniency: rating too high to keep people happy.

The best protection is notes. Ask managers to jot down examples, good and bad, through the year. A review built on examples is much harder to argue with.

Running the conversation

  1. Prepare: goals, evidence, the self-assessment, attendance and any feedback.
  2. Start by asking how they think the year went.
  3. Talk about specific examples, not personality.
  4. Cover what went well before what needs to improve, and be honest about both.
  5. Agree two or three goals for the next period and any training needed.
  6. Write it down, and give the employee a copy.

Reviews for a large site workforce

For hundreds of blue-collar workers, a long form does not work. Use a one-page form with a few clear criteria such as attendance, safety, quality of work and teamwork. Let the foreman or supervisor complete it, and HR check for consistency. Where possible, hold the conversation in a language the worker is comfortable with. It makes a real difference to how the feedback lands.

Keep pay and development in view

If ratings are linked to increments or bonuses, say so openly and explain how. Just as important, use the review to plan training and next steps. People take reviews more seriously when they can see it leads somewhere.

A good review should feel like a summary of conversations you have already had during the year. If it feels like a surprise, the problem is not the form. It is the months before it.
